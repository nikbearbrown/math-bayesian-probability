Introduction to Probability

In this chapter, we explore how to quantify beliefs using
probability, which allows for a numerical understanding of
uncertainty. Assigning numbers to beliefs enables us to
create models that show how evidence influences our
thinking and helps us determine when to update our beliefs.

What is a Probability?

Probability reflects the strength of our beliefs about events. It
extends basic logic, which relies on absolute true or false
values, to accommodate uncertainty. Probabilities range from
0 (false) to 1 (true), with a value of 0.5 indicating total
uncertainty. The negation of an event follows the principle
that the probability of an event and its negation sum to 1.

Calculating Probabilities by Counting Outcomes

The standard approach to calculating probability involves
counting the outcomes of an event. A basic example is a coin
toss, which has two possible outcomes: heads or tails. The




                                                         Scan to Download
probability for heads is calculated as the ratio of the desired
outcome to the total outcomes (P(heads) = 1/2). More
complex questions, like the probability of getting at least one
heads in two tosses, can be determined through similar
counting, where combinatorial analysis may be needed for
more challenging scenarios.

Calculating Probabilities as Ratios of Beliefs

For more abstract probabilities, like predicting rain or
assessing claims like the Mandela effect, counting outcomes
is impractical. Instead, beliefs can be quantified through
betting odds. For instance, if you would bet $100 against
your friend’s $5 on the existence of a Wikipedia article about
the Mandela effect, these odds represent your relative beliefs
and can be converted to probabilities.

Using Odds to Determine Probability

Odds express the ratio of the strength of belief in one
hypothesis over another. The example provided calculates
the probability that there is no article on the Mandela effect
using a simple equation derived from these odds. Eventually,
the results yield a probability between 0 and 1, which




                                                        Scan to Download
combines both hypotheses and adheres to the foundational
rules of probability.

Measuring Beliefs in a Coin Toss

By applying the same ratio method used for the Mandela
effect to a fair coin toss, we can confirm that our belief about
the probability of heads (P(Hheads)) and tails (P(Htails))
remains equal, yielding P(Hheads) = 1/2, consistent with our
earlier counting approach.

Conclusion

This chapter reviews two methods for calculating
probabilities—one for discrete events and another for
abstract beliefs—ultimately highlighting their equivalence.
The framework provided enables quantifying beliefs across
various scenarios, which will be built upon in the following
chapters. Exercise questions help reinforce the concepts
learned.

Understanding the Binomial Distribution

The binomial distribution is characterized by three
parameters:
-
k
: Number of successful outcomes of interest
-
n
: Total number of trials
-
p
: Probability of success on a single trial
The binomial distribution can be notated as B(k; n, p), where
specific instances, such as flipping two heads in three coin
tosses, can be represented as B(2; 3, 1/2).

Generalizing Probability Problems

To create a function that generalizes the binomial




                                                         Scan to Download
distribution, we can start by identifying the number of
desired outcomes (k), total trials (n), and the probability of
success (p). Using combinatory mathematics, the binomial
coefficient (n choose k) helps in determining the number of
ways to achieve k successes in n trials.

Probability of Desired Outcome

The probability of any specific outcome is computed using
the product rule and negation, leading to the general binomial
distribution formula:
\[ B(k; n, p) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k} \]
where \( \binom{n}{k} \) is the binomial coefficient.

Introduction to Bayes' Theorem

In this chapter, we explore Bayes' theorem as a valuable tool
for reasoning about uncertainty and quantifying beliefs based
on observed data. The three components of Bayes'
theorem—posterior probability, likelihood, and prior
probability—are essential for this analysis.

The Three Parts of Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem quantifies the change in our beliefs based on
new data, represented as P(belief | data). The three
components include:
1.
Posterior Probability (P(belief | data))
: The strength of our belief given the observed data.
2.
Likelihood (P(data | belief))
: The probability of observing the data given our belief.
3.
Prior Probability (P(belief))
: The initial strength of our belief before observing the data.

Investigating a Crime Scenario




                                                        Scan to Download
To illustrate Bayes' theorem, we analyze a scenario where
one returns home to find signs of a possible burglary. The
hypotheses and their corresponding probabilities are
established to evaluate the likelihood of being robbed.

Calculating the Likelihood

For the hypothesis of being robbed, we assess how probable
the observed evidence—broken window, open door, and
missing laptop—would be given that robbery occurred. A
preliminary estimate leads to a likelihood of 3/10.

Determining the Prior

The prior probability is influenced by contextual factors,
such as the crime rate in the neighborhood. For the current
scenario, we set a prior probability of 1/11,000 for being
robbed.

Normalizing the Data

To arrive at a valid posterior probability, we need to consider
the total probability of observing the evidence (P(D)).

11 Measuring the Spread of Our Data

In this chapter, you’ll learn three different methods—mean
absolute deviation, variance, and standard deviation—for
quantifying the spread of observations. The spread of data
relates to the uncertainty in estimating the mean.

Dropping Coins in a Well

The chapter starts with an example of dropping coins in a
well to measure how long it takes for them to hit the bottom.
Two groups of observations are collected, and although both
groups have similar means, their spreads differ. This
demonstrates the need for quantifying the spread to
understand uncertainty in measurement.

Finding the Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD)

To measure spread, the distance of each observation from the




                                                      Scan to Download
mean is calculated. Summing these differences is not helpful
since positive and negative differences can cancel each other
out. Instead, absolute values are used, leading to the mean
absolute deviation (MAD):
\[ \text{MAD} = \frac{1}{n} \sum |x_i - \mu| \]
MAD is intuitive and shows group b being approximately 10
times more spread out than group a.

Finding the Variance

Another way to express spread is by calculating the variance,
which involves squaring the differences from the mean:
\[ \text{Var} = \frac{1}{n} \sum (x_i - \mu)^2 \]
Variance is mathematically easier to handle but can be less
intuitive since it represents the average squared difference,
leading to potentially counterintuitive large values when
comparing spreads.

Finding the Standard Deviation

To improve interpretability, the square root of the variance is
taken, resulting in the standard deviation (Ã):
\[ \sigma = \sqrt{\text{Var}} \]
The standard deviation provides results that closely resemble




                                                        Scan to Download
the MAD, maintaining an intuitive understanding of spread.

Summary of Methods

The chapter concludes with a comparison of the three
methods for measuring spread:
- Mean Absolute Deviation: Intuitive, but less
mathematically useful.
- Variance: Mathematically preferred but lacks intuitive
clarity.
- Standard Deviation: Balances mathematical use and
intuitive understanding.

Bayes' theorem provides a framework for updating our

-
P(H | D)

-

This helps to establish how confident we are in each
hypothesis before considering new data.
- Finally, we derive
posterior odds

2.Question
What is the product rule of probability and how does it
apply when combining events?
Answer:The product rule states that for two events A and B,




                                                         Scan to Download
the probability of both events occurring (A AND B) is
calculated by multiplying their individual probabilities: P(A,
B) = P(A) × P(B). This rule allows us to determine the
chance of related events happening together by considering
their probabilities separately.

3.Question
Can you give an example of applying the product rule to a
real-life scenario?
Answer:Sure! Imagine you're catching a bus and a train to
meet a friend. If the probability of the bus arriving late is 0.2
and the train is 0.15, the probability that both are late (thus
making you late) is calculated using the product rule: P(Late
bus, Late train) = 0.2 × 0.15 = 0.03, which means there's a
3% chance of being late.

4.Question
What distinguishes mutually exclusive from non-mutually
exclusive events when using the OR probability rule?
Answer:Mutually exclusive events cannot occur at the same
time (e.g., flipping heads or tails on a coin); their combined




                                                          Scan to Download
probability is simply the sum of their individual
probabilities: P(A OR B) = P(A) + P(B). In contrast,
non-mutually exclusive events can occur together (like being
sick or raining when considering a canceled event), and we
have to subtract the probability of both occurring
simultaneously to avoid double-counting: P(A OR B) = P(A)
+ P(B) - P(A, B).

5.Question
What does the sum rule of probability entail, especially
for non-mutually exclusive events?
Answer:For non-mutually exclusive events, the sum rule
states that to find the probability of either event A or event B
occurring, we add their individual probabilities and subtract
the probability of both occurring: P(A OR B) = P(A) + P(B) -
P(A, B). This corrects for any double-counting of the
occurrences that could happen simultaneously.

(missing is 0.8), you calculate the combined probability of
missing either using the sum rule, accounting for any
potential overlap with the product rule to find the probability
