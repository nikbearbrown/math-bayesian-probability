## Chapter Anatomy Template

Course-textbook anatomy. Every primary chapter (1–13) follows this 7-part structure:

1. **The Problem** — one concrete situation, stated before any theory
2. **Frequentist Solution** — built and interpreted completely
3. **Where Frequentist Strains** — the specific failure mode for this problem
4. **Bayesian Solution** — built and interpreted completely
5. **Side-by-Side Comparison** — assumptions, capabilities, limits of each
6. **Prompting for Implementation** — how to prompt an LLM for this chapter's analysis (assumes Ch 2)
7. **Exercises** — ≥3, at least one requiring production, at least one at Apply+ under both frameworks

**Asymmetry rule:** from Act Two on, chapters give more space to the Bayesian
solution because the frequentist analog is simpler. Named explicitly first at
Ch 5, treated as evidence not apology. Ch 2 and Ch 12 deviate from the template
(methods chapter; guided project).

## Chapter Specifications (condensed)

### Chapter 0 — Probability Foundations
- **One-line:** everything needed before Ch 1 — conditional probability, Bayes' theorem as arithmetic, nothing more.
- **Opening:** direct problem statement (prerequisite resolver, not a template chapter).
- **Content:** joint probability & multiplication rule; P(A|B)=P(A∩B)/P(B); the P(A|B)≠P(B|A) asymmetry; Bayes as arithmetic; two hand calculations.
- **Worked example:** urn (3 red, 7 blue), "it's not blue — P(red)?" solved by formula.
- **Exit condition:** reader can compute two-hypothesis posteriors by hand before opening Ch 1. No prompting section (arithmetic only).

### Chapter 1 — The Same Question, Two Answers
- **One-line:** the medical-test problem reveals what NHST can't say and introduces the Bayesian alternative.
- **Opening:** failure/paradox — "99% accurate test, positive result; most say 99%; it isn't."
- **Worked example:** disease prevalence 0.001, sensitivity/specificity 0.99 → posterior ≈ 9%.
- **Bridge:** the Bayesian calc needs the prior the test ignores → Ch 2 teaches implementation.

### Chapter 2 — Prompting for Statistics
- **One-line:** use an LLM as a statistical implementation partner — describe precisely, verify critically, iterate.
- **Opening:** the open implementation problem left by Ch 1.
- **Content:** why prompting for stats is different; anatomy of a good statistical prompt; finishing Ch 1's open problem; the comparison-prompt template; failure modes (right code/wrong model, right model/wrong interpretation, frequentist-in-Bayesian-clothing, missing prior).
- **Worked example:** full prompting session shown as dialogue, with one interpretation-error iteration. Methods chapter — no freq/Bayes comparison section.

### Chapter 3 — Counting and Estimating
- **One-line:** the binomial/proportion problem both ways; CI vs. credible interval at its clearest.
- **Worked example:** 8 defective of 50 boards; freq CI [0.065, 0.255] vs. Beta(9,43) posterior, P(rate<0.20) directly computable.
- **Bridge:** conjugacy closes the math here; Ch 4 adds a second group and the closure starts to break.

### Chapter 4 — Comparing Two Groups
- **One-line:** the t-test and its Bayesian analog; first encounter with why significant results don't replicate.
- **Worked example:** tutorial A (n=40, 72%) vs. B (n=38, 76%); p=0.03 vs. P(B better)=0.91; Ioannidis replication sidebar.
- **Bridge:** simple models so far; Ch 5 adds a continuous predictor.

### Chapter 5 — Regression, Both Ways *(transition chapter; asymmetry named here)*
- **One-line:** OLS vs. Bayesian regression — lines converge, decision-relevant outputs diverge.
- **Worked example:** sales vs. ad spend over 60 weeks; slope 2.3; posterior predictive vs. prediction interval; P(ROI>0) only Bayesian.
- **Bridge:** regression assumes we know the model; Ch 6 compares models.

### Chapter 6 — Model Comparison
- **One-line:** AIC/LRT (ranking) vs. Bayes factors (probability); the gap matters most when models are close.
- **Worked example:** epidemic linear vs. exponential; ΔAIC=4.2 vs. BF=8.3; model averaging; what to do when BF≈1.
- **Bridge:** comparison assumes the right models; Ch 7 surfaces the hidden assumptions — the priors.

### Chapter 7 — Priors
- **One-line:** every analysis has priors; frequentist hides them, Bayesian names them. Same data, three priors.
- **Fairness test (named):** frequentist prior-free analysis is a real advantage in novel settings and regulated environments (FDA/EMA) — not a strawman.
- **Worked example:** one clinical-trial dataset under uniform / weakly-informative / informative priors; tails move, point estimate barely does; prior-sensitivity analysis.
- **Bridge:** priors do the most work when data is sparse → Ch 8.

### Chapter 8 — When Data Is Sparse
- **One-line:** small samples, rare events, underpowered studies; the winner's curse and Bayesian shrinkage as regularization.
- **Worked example:** 3 complications in 200 procedures; freq CI [0.003,0.043] vs. literature prior Beta(3,200) → Beta(6,397); winner's-curse explanation (Bernoulli's fallacy in practice).
- **Bridge:** observation-level sparsity → grouped-structure sparsity in Ch 9.

### Chapter 9 — Hierarchical Problems *(hardest chapter; opens with asymmetry notice)*
- **One-line:** grouped data and partial pooling; the sharpest divergence and most pronounced asymmetry.
- **Worked example:** 30 schools, sizes 8–200; complete vs. no vs. partial pooling; mixed-effects vs. full Bayesian hierarchical posteriors. Longer prompting section.
- **Bridge:** grouped structure in cross-section → structure in time, Ch 10.

### Chapter 10 — Time and Sequence
- **One-line:** time series and sequential updating — today's posterior is tomorrow's prior.
- **Worked example:** 52 weeks of demand; ARIMA point forecast vs. Bayesian structural time series; sequential-updating visualization; P(demand>threshold).
- **Bridge:** sequential decisions → classification decisions, Ch 11.

### Chapter 11 — Classification and Decision
- **One-line:** classification as inference under a loss function; the threshold is a decision under costs.
- **Worked example:** loan default; logistic regression + ROC vs. Bayesian logistic with posterior predictive; threshold from explicit asymmetric loss. **Irreducibly Human link:** O*NET automation-exposure classification.
- **Bridge:** toolkit complete → turn it loose on a real dataset, Ch 12.

### Chapter 12 — A Real Problem, Both Ways *(guided capstone, not a lecture chapter)*
- **One-line:** one real dataset, one real question, both frameworks; deliverable is a written comparison, not a winner.
- **Structure:** dataset-selection guide + selection prompt; analysis scaffold (the six required questions); a halfway worked example; a writing guide.
- **Deliverable:** 4–6 page written comparative analysis answering the six questions. Datasets from BLS/O*NET on the companion site. No traditional exercises — the chapter is the exercise.

### Chapter 13 — Choosing
- **One-line:** a framework for selecting an approach by problem, data, decision, and audience — not a verdict.
- **Content:** when frequentist is right; when Bayesian earns its cost; the 5-question decision framework; what the book did not cover (honest pointers); metacognitive close ("statistics can inform judgments, not replace them").

## Hard Chapters

1. **Chapter 9 — Hierarchical Problems.** Conceptually densest; biggest freq/Bayes asymmetry; specifying hyperpriors to an LLM is error-prone. Risk if done poorly: the reader loses the "what the complexity buys" payoff. Ideal author: someone who has fit both mixed-effects and hierarchical Bayesian models in practice.
2. **Chapter 7 — Priors.** Must be even-handed — the chapter that most risks becoming Bayesian advocacy. Needs the fairness test to land.
3. **Chapter 2 — Prompting for Statistics.** Highest aging risk; must teach a durable verification skill, not tool-specific tricks.
